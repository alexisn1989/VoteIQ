The Gatekeeper Effect
Virginia's Top Real Estate Donors Gave Millions — and Sponsored Zero Bills
Commercial Real Estate is the sector with the most negative correlation between donation amounts and bill sponsorship (r = −0.19, p < 0.05). The biggest donors don't fund bill champions — they fund caucus leaders and committee chairs who control which bills ever reach a vote. Influence exercised through scheduling, agenda-setting, and committee assignments leaves no public paper trail.
The finding: Virginia's top 10 Commercial Real Estate donors collectively gave — to state legislators in the SBE dataset. Those same 10 legislators sponsored a combined — real estate bills. The money went to gatekeepers, not sponsors.
What leadership money actually buys: Committee chairmanships control which bills receive hearings. A single committee chair can table a tenant-protection bill without a single floor vote. The donor never needs to ask for a specific "no" — they just need to fund the person who decides what the full chamber ever gets to see.

Party Breakdown
Party Identity Explains More Than Dollar Amount
After controlling for sector, party affiliation is a far stronger predictor of legislative behavior than the size of donations. Every legislator whose votes diverge from their top donor's interests is a Democrat. No Republican in our dataset shows a divergence score below 50%. This doesn't mean Republicans are "bought" — it means their ideology already aligned with their donors before the money arrived. For Democrats, some take industry money while maintaining progressive records.
The investment model vs. the purchase model: Political science distinguishes two theories of donor behavior. The investment model (Goldstein, 1999; Ansolabehere et al., 2003) holds that donors back legislators who already agree with them — ideology comes first. The purchase model holds that donations cause changed legislative behavior. Our data is consistent with the investment model: party identity predicts bill sponsorship far better than donation amounts do. The 8 "strongly opposed" Democratic cases are legislators whose base constituency pulls them further left than their donor mix would predict.

What "opposed" actually means: A 0% alignment score does not mean a legislator is corrupt or naive — it means their legislative record is orthogonal to (or actively counter to) that donor's policy interests. Dave Marsden takes fossil fuel money and sponsors clean energy legislation. Kathy Tran takes corporate law money and sponsors class action expansion. These are legislators whose progressive base is strong enough that they can afford to take broad-market donations without changing their behavior.
Contribution Timing
Session Money vs. Campaign Money — A Different Signal
Virginia's General Assembly meets January–March. Contributions that arrive during that window — while committee hearings are active and floor votes are scheduled — carry a different meaning than fundraiser checks written in October. A session premium above 1.0× means the average check written during session is larger than the average off-season check: lobbyists are paying an access premium when it matters most. A ratio below 1.0× is equally revealing — those industries give continuously throughout the year, buying ongoing relationships rather than timing specific votes.
Average check size: session (Jan–Mar) vs. off-season — corporate & PAC contributions only, 2018+
Renewables: 1.76× session premium — Clean energy lobbyists write checks 76% larger during the legislative session than during campaigns. This is the strongest timing signal in the data: these donors are buying access at the moment solar permitting and clean energy mandate bills are in committee.
Gambling/Casinos (0.89×) and Commercial Real Estate (0.96×) give less per check during session. These industries maintain continuous year-round relationships rather than timing contributions to specific hearings. Their influence operates through permanent access — not episodic pressure.

Data sources & methodology
Donor data from Virginia State Board of Elections (SBE) Schedule A filings, 2023–2025 cycles, classified into industries by employer/occupation keyword matching. Bill data from LegiScan VA 2025–2026 session (7,149 bills); industries tagged by title/summary keyword matching. Correlation method: Pearson r on (log₁₀ donated, bills sponsored). Log scale used because donation distributions are right-skewed across 3 orders of magnitude. Bill weight: Primary sponsorship = 1.0, co-sponsorship = 0.5. Alignment score: Weighted ratio of aligned vs. opposed legislative actions (sponsor = 3×, cosponsor = 2×, vote = 1×) using cross-sector bill tagging.

Honest limitations: This analysis captures only publicly traceable actions — sponsorships and floor votes. The most consequential legislative influence — committee scheduling, amendment negotiations, regulatory appointments, caucus messaging — leaves no public paper trail and cannot be measured here. Correlations are observational. This analysis does not infer causation, improper influence, or intent. The presence of industry donations does not imply that a legislator acted improperly.
